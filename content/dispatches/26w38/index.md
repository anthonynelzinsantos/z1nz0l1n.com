I’m sure the [iPhone Duo](https://www.apple.com/iphone-duo/) will be a perfectly fine device, but having reviewed my fair share of two-handed foldable phones, i just don’t get the hype. You might believe iOS will make all the difference, and i have to admit that the incredibly smooth opening animation might push people to actually unfold their device, something that Samsung Galaxy Z Fold and Honor Magic V6 owners might not do every single day. But i believe books, both paper- and silicon-based, have something to teach us about what the hand wants.

The oldest electronic reading device i own, the Franklin Electronic Publishers KJ-21 electronic Bible from 1989, could claim to have been the first e-reader. Like the Sony Data Discman DD-1, which followed a year later and is slowly turning back to petroleum in my junk closet, it featured a keyboard for easy information retrieval. The first e-readers you’d recognize as such arrived in 1998 — the NuvoMedia Rocket eBook, which introduced the now-familiar asymmetrical design with page turning buttons, and the SoftBook Reader, which already had a built-in storefront thanks to its integrated modem. Both ran on power-hungry LCD screens, but E Ink arrived in 2004, with the launch of the Sony LIBRIe EBR-1000EP in Japan.

Back in 1949, Ángela Ruiz Robles’s [mechanical encyclopedia](https://portal.mineco.gob.es/es-es/ministerio/publicaciones/electronicas/Paginas/Angela_Ruiz_Robles_y_la_invencion_del_libro_mecanico.aspx) looked a lot like a book, but fifty years later, these so-called electronic books looked nothing like their paper counterparts. The [codex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex) might be the most powerful device ever invented, but its main drawback is that it requires two hands[^1]. The continuous success of e-readers stems in large part from their one-handedness. You can use them in the metro while holding on to the overhead handles, in the street while gulping your cup of industrial coffee, in the office while munching on your stale sandwich… well, you get the idea.

The sudden popularity of tiny readers might have been a Reddit-driven psyop[^2], but they’re clearly beloved because they fit so well in the hand. You don’t see a lot of people clamouring for foldable e-readers — Readmoo’s Mooink V is vapourware at this point. Even note-taking devices have shrunk to a palm-sized format — except for the [InkLeaf](https://useinkleaf.com), which might be the daftest device i’ve ever seen, and i’ve held a [Humane AI Pin](https://www.theverge.com/24126502/humane-ai-pin-review). You might say that phones are infinitely more versatile devices and that you want to be able to use two apps side by side. But are you going to? *Really?* The e-reader didn’t succeed because two-handed reading was impossible. It succeeded because, given the choice, people stopped choosing it. What choice will iPhone Duo owners make in the long run?

The iPhone Duo reminds me of another device that shared the same moniker, the ill-fated [PowerBook Duo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PowerBook_Duo). Hardcore technologists have always dreamed of a single device that could transform and adapt to different use cases. Be it the IBM PC Convertible, the Compaq SLT/286, the Nokia 9000 Communicator or the Motorola Atrix, not to mention the cancelled Palm Foleo and Ubuntu Edge, the result has always been lower than the sum of its parts. Will it be different with the iPhone Duo? It’ll sell, because it’s Apple, but it might stay mostly closed, because it’s a phone (and one that can’t fit comfortably in one hand even when closed). In the meantime, when i need a screen bigger than my smartphone’s, i’ll reach for the second most powerful device ever invented, the bag. I’m sure i’ll find something in there.

[^1]: Insert a joke about “those books one reads with one hand” at your own risk.
[^2]: Let me have my harmless conspiracy theories.
